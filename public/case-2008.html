Case study · Hypothetical backtest

2008, in slow motion.

The financial crisis ended a 25-year bull market. The S&P 500 lost 38%. QQQ lost 41%. Trillions of dollars in retirement savings evaporated, much of it never to be recovered by the people who were within five years of using it. Here's how a rules-based rotation strategy navigated the year, hypothetically — without predicting anything.

The setup, late 2007

The QQQ closed 2007 at $51.12. Tech had been the leadership sector for years. Apple was launching the first iPhone, Google was minting money, the housing slowdown was theoretically contained to subprime, and the consensus view among economists was that any recession would be mild and brief.

The algorithm doesn't care about consensus views. It doesn't read Fed minutes. It doesn't have an opinion on subprime mortgages or Bear Stearns. What it does is watch a few specific things:

Price relative to its 200-day moving average. Volume confirmation through volume signal. Trend strength via shorter-term moving averages. When certain combinations of these signals fire together, the algorithm concludes the equity regime has shifted and rotates accordingly.

Entering 2008, the algorithm was fully long QQQ. The regime was technically still bullish — price above the 200-day moving average, trend intact. That changed on January 18th.

The five rotations of 2008

January 18, 2008
QQQ → TLT
QQQ closed below its 200-day moving average for the first time since 2003. volume signal crossover confirmed selling pressure. The algorithm rotated to TLT — long-term Treasuries, which were themselves in a bullish regime as bond yields fell on flight-to-safety flows.
March 26, 2008
TLT → QQQ (brief)
After the Bear Stearns rescue, equity markets rallied sharply. QQQ briefly reclaimed its 200-day average. The algorithm re-entered. This turned out to be a whipsaw — one of the uncomfortable realities of any trend-following system.
June 23, 2008
QQQ → TLT
As oil hit $140 and consumer sentiment collapsed, QQQ lost the 200-day again. This time it stayed gone. The rotation to TLT caught the beginning of the second leg of the bear market.
October 2008 — held through
Hold TLT
During the most violent weeks of the crisis — Lehman, AIG, TARP, the stock-market dislocations of October — the algorithm sat in TLT. Treasuries rallied sharply as global capital fled to perceived safety. This is the period that did most of the heavy lifting on the year's return.
December 12, 2008
TLT → CASH
In December, TLT itself broke below its 200-day moving average as Treasury yields began to back up from extreme lows. The algorithm doesn't hold defensive assets when those assets are themselves in a downtrend. It moved to cash and finished the year there.

The math of 2008

The hypothetical strategy finished 2008 up approximately 27.66%. Buy-and-hold QQQ finished the year down approximately 41.73%. The difference — roughly 67 percentage points on the year — is most of what makes the long-term backtest look the way it does.

This isn't because the algorithm is clever. It's because the rules for entering and exiting are simple, and because being out of equities during the worst calendar year for equities in seventy years turns out to matter enormously to compounding outcomes.

A $100,000 portfolio that started 2008 in QQQ ended the year at about $59,000 if it just held. The hypothetical Ocean Blue portfolio ended at about $126,000. The relative wealth difference of more than 2x at the end of one year compounds across every subsequent year of market exposure.

What went right (and what could have gone wrong)

It would be easy to read this case study and conclude that the algorithm "saw the crisis coming." It didn't. The algorithm doesn't predict. It exited QQQ in January 2008 because price action confirmed a regime shift — not because it understood anything about credit markets or housing.

If the crisis had been narrower — say, a normal recession with a 20% equity drawdown — the algorithm would still have rotated, would still have produced something close to a flat year, and would have given up some of the eventual recovery. That's the trade-off. Rotation strategies don't time bottoms well. The cost of avoiding the bottom is that you often miss part of the rebound.

The reason 2008 looks so favorable for the algorithm is precisely because the year was so unusually bad. In a more typical bear market, the strategy would have meaningfully reduced losses but wouldn't have produced a positive year. The dynamic that allowed +26% in 2008 was the simultaneous rally in long-term Treasuries — a feature of the crisis that doesn't always occur in equity drawdowns.

The 2009 cost

Honesty requires acknowledging the other half. The algorithm produced about 29% in 2009. Buy-and-hold QQQ produced about 47%. The strategy was still partially in TLT for the first quarter of 2009 — the worst period to be out of equities, as it turned out — and didn't fully re-enter QQQ until later in the year.

This is the structural cost of trend-following: you miss bottoms. By the time price action has confirmed that the regime has shifted back to bullish, a significant portion of the recovery has already happened. In 2009, that cost about 18 percentage points of relative performance.

Over the full 2008-2009 cycle, the strategy still came out ahead of buy-and-hold by a meaningful margin. The 67-point edge in 2008 was larger than the 18-point lag in 2009. But the lag was real, and it's the kind of thing that tests subscriber patience in the months it happens.

What this case study is and isn't

It's a transparent walkthrough of how the rules would have applied to one famous year, given the data that was available at the time. Five rotations. No predictions. No clever discretion. The algorithm reacted to what price was actually doing, and the outcome over that particular twelve months looked like this.

It isn't a guarantee of anything. The next equity drawdown may not look like 2008. Treasuries may not rally — they didn't in 2022, which is a case study for a different day. The algorithm may generate signals that turn out to be wrong, and you'll be holding whatever it told you to hold while that wrong signal unwinds.

What we can say is that for one specific terrible year for equity investors, a simple set of rules applied without emotion or prediction produced an outcome that most discretionary investors would have envied. That's the kind of evidence that matters: not that the strategy is always right, but that when it works, it works for understandable reasons.
