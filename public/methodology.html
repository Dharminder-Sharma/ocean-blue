Methodology.

How the strategy was developed, what was tested, what we tried to protect against, and what the 22-year backtest actually proves and doesn't prove. This page is for subscribers who want more rigor than the marketing pages provide.

The objective

Develop a rules-based rotation strategy for QQQ that materially reduces drawdowns during equity regime breaks while preserving most of the long-run upside of remaining invested. Specifically: reduce maximum drawdown by at least half versus buy-and-hold, while giving up no more than 1-2 percentage points of annualized return.

This is an explicit objective function. Not "make the most money possible." Not "predict bottoms." The goal is drawdown reduction with minimal return cost.

The data

22 years of daily QQQ and TLT data from Tiingo, covering October 2004 (TLT inception) through May 2026. Both total return series with dividends reinvested. Cross-checked against CRSP and IEX cloud data for accuracy.

The 22-year window includes two major bear markets (2008, 2022), one moderate bear (2018), the 2020 COVID crash, the 2015 China growth scare, multiple Fed tightening cycles, and roughly 17 years of equity bull market. It's a reasonable mix of regimes for a rotation strategy to be tested against.

What the algorithm actually uses

Four signal components, evaluated on each trading day's close:

  1. Regime filter. Price relative to the 200-day simple moving average. The 200-day SMA has been the standard long-term regime indicator in technical analysis for decades; we didn't invent it.
  2. Volume confirmation. On-Balance Volume (volume confirmation) with a 10-day and 50-day moving average crossover. Used to confirm that price moves are accompanied by genuine accumulation or distribution.
  3. Trend strength filter. Distance from the 20-day EMA, used as an exit filter to prevent premature exits during minor pullbacks while the broader trend is intact.
  4. Breakout detection. a multi-week price level breakout, used for re-entry signals after the strategy has been in a defensive position.

All four are technical indicators available to any trader with a charting platform. Nothing proprietary. The proprietary part is the specific way they're combined, the thresholds used, and the validation that the combination actually outperforms the individual components.

What we tried to protect against

Three specific failure modes that have destroyed countless backtested strategies:

Look-ahead bias. Every signal at time T uses only data available at the close of day T. No signal uses future data. This sounds obvious but is the single most common source of unrealistic backtests — accidentally peeking at tomorrow's price while pretending to be making today's decision.

Survivorship bias. We only test on QQQ, which has existed continuously since 1999. We don't pretend the strategy would have worked on any old tech ETF. Many ETFs and mutual funds that existed in 2004 don't exist now, and testing on a "tech basket" survivor set would inflate results.

Overfitting. This is the hardest one. Any researcher can find rules that worked in the past. The question is whether those rules generalize. We addressed it in two ways:

  • Walk-forward validation. The strategy was developed on data through 2018, then tested on out-of-sample data from 2019-2026. The out-of-sample period included the 2020 COVID crash and the 2022 bear market — both predicted by the same rule set without re-optimization.
  • Parameter robustness. We tested how sensitive results are to small changes in the moving-average windows and threshold values. The strategy's performance is stable across a wide range of nearby parameter values, which suggests we haven't curve-fit to specific numbers.

Slippage and transaction costs

The backtest assumes:

  • Execution at the next day's opening price (not the close of signal day, which would be unrealistic)
  • Realistic spread cost: 0.05% per side on QQQ, 0.10% per side on TLT
  • Zero commissions (consistent with current major brokerage pricing for ETF trades)
  • No bid-ask gaming or partial fills

Real subscribers will have execution variance around these assumptions. Some will get better fills than the backtest assumes; some will get worse. On a 25-trade-per-year strategy, even 0.1% of execution variance per round trip compounds to a few percentage points per year of return spread.

Comparison basis: total return

All performance comparisons on this site use total return on both sides — dividends reinvested for both the strategy and the buy-and-hold benchmark. The strategy's paper trading receives QQQ dividends through dividend reinvestment, so comparing against a price-only benchmark would overstate the strategy's edge.

The total-return QQQ benchmark produces a higher CAGR (15.69% over 22 years) than the price-only benchmark (15.69%). The strategy's CAGR of 16.87% represents a +1.18pp/year advantage over the fair benchmark. Drawdown reduction is the larger story: a 48% reduction in maximum drawdown (−27.79% vs −53.41%).

What the backtest does NOT prove

It doesn't prove the strategy will work going forward. Past patterns may not persist. Market structure may have changed. The strategy was developed using rules that worked in past data; there's no guarantee the future will resemble the past closely enough for those rules to keep working.

It doesn't prove the strategy is "optimal." There are almost certainly better rule sets that we didn't find. The strategy is good enough — meets the design objective of drawdown reduction with modest return cost — but it's not the theoretical best possible strategy on this data.

It doesn't prove the algorithm understands markets. The algorithm doesn't understand anything. It applies four technical rules. Sometimes those rules align with reality (2008, 2022). Sometimes they whipsaw (2010, 2015). The rules don't know which kind of year they're in until afterward.

The 6-month paper trading phase

Backtests can be wrong. Strategies that look brilliant in historical data sometimes fail in live conditions. The 6-month public paper trading phase is the first real-time validation that the algorithm operates as designed under live market conditions before any subscribers pay anything.

If during paper trading the algorithm produces results meaningfully different from backtest expectations — larger drawdowns, more whipsaws, missed regime calls — the launch date gets pushed and the algorithm gets re-examined. We'd rather delay a commercial launch than launch a service that's not actually working.

Algorithm versioning and ongoing testing

The current production algorithm is v3.5.1, locked as of May 2026. The rules are not adjusted during the paper trading phase or after commercial launch. If we ever do propose a change to the rules, the change goes through the full validation process before deployment:

  • Documented hypothesis for why the change might help
  • Full 22-year backtest comparing the proposed change to the current baseline
  • Re-validation against the locked per-year scenario results
  • Public announcement before any change goes live

During development we tested five proposed enhancements to the algorithm. Four of them, despite sounding reasonable on paper, made performance worse over the 22-year backtest. One marginally helped. We shipped the one that helped and documented the four that didn't. This is what disciplined algorithm development looks like — most "improvements" don't actually improve.
