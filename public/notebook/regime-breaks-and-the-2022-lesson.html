Regime breaks and the 2022 lesson

2022 was the worst year on record for the standard retirement portfolio. Stocks fell. Bonds fell. The thing that's supposed to protect you from one falling was falling at the same time. Here's why our algorithm sat most of it out, and what the year actually proved.

Most years, the 60/40 retirement portfolio does what it's supposed to do. Stocks zig, bonds zag. When one is having a bad year the other usually isn't, and the blend produces a smoother ride than holding either one alone. That's the whole pitch of the asset class diversification model that's powered the retirement industry for fifty years.

Then 2022 happened. The S&P 500 fell about 18%. Long-term bonds — specifically TLT — fell about 30%. A traditional 60/40 portfolio finished the year down something like 17%, the worst calendar year for that allocation since at least the 1930s. Investors who'd been told their whole adult lives that "bonds protect you when stocks fall" watched both sides of their portfolio bleed at the same time.

−17.5% The 2022 return of a 60% stocks / 40% bonds portfolio. Stocks and bonds fell together — for the first time in roughly four decades.

What our algorithm did in 2022

We exited QQQ in mid-January. The algorithm doesn't predict, doesn't forecast, doesn't read economic releases. What it does is watch a few specific price and volume relationships, and when those relationships shift in a particular way, it tells you the regime has changed.

In January 2022, the regime changed. Price broke below its long-term moving average. Volume confirmed. The algorithm rotated to TLT briefly, then — because TLT itself was also below its long-term moving average, meaning the bond market was in its own regime break — moved to cash and stayed there for most of the year.

That's the part people sometimes get wrong about rotation strategies. The strategy isn't predicting where the market is going. It's reading where the market is right now, and refusing to participate in regimes that historically have produced bad outcomes. The reading isn't always correct in real time. But over a long enough sample, "I refuse to hold this thing while its long-term moving average is sloping down" turns out to be a useful rule.

The bond-correlation problem

Here's the thing most allocation models don't admit: the negative correlation between stocks and bonds that justifies the 60/40 portfolio isn't a law of physics. It's a feature of the specific economic regime we lived in from roughly 1985 to 2020 — disinflation, declining interest rates, central bank credibility.

When that regime ended in 2022 — inflation broke out, rates rose rapidly, bond prices collapsed — stocks and bonds suddenly became positively correlated. They fell together. The protection mechanism failed exactly when investors needed it most.

This isn't a problem with the 60/40 portfolio specifically. It's a problem with any static allocation that assumes the future will resemble the past. The defensive asset is only defensive while the correlation holds. When the correlation breaks, the defense becomes an additional liability.

What rotation actually does differently

A rules-based rotation strategy doesn't pretend to know which correlation regime we're in. It checks every day. If TLT is acting like a defensive asset (above its long-term moving average, in an uptrend), fine — rotate there when leaving QQQ. If TLT is itself in a downtrend, don't pretend it's defensive. Go to cash instead.

It's not a complicated idea. It just requires being willing to sit in cash for months at a time, which feels deeply wrong if you've been told for years that "time in the market beats timing the market." Sometimes that's true. In 2022, "time in cash" beat "time in the market" by roughly twenty percentage points.

−8.19% vs. −33.71% Our hypothetical 2022 result, versus buy-and-hold QQQ for the same year. Roughly 25 percentage points of capital preserved through one application of the regime filter.

What 2022 didn't prove

It would be tempting to use a year like 2022 to claim the strategy is somehow superior to buy-and-hold. We try hard not to do that. Here's what 2022 actually proved:

It proved the regime filter does what it's supposed to do. Not that it always will. Not that it can't whipsaw badly in choppy markets — it did in 2010, 2015, and to a lesser extent 2019. But when a real regime break happens, the algorithm catches it without needing anyone to make a prediction.

It proved that the standard retirement protection mechanism can fail. Bonds didn't help in 2022. Diversification across asset classes didn't help. Investors holding traditional balanced portfolios lost real purchasing power on top of the nominal drawdown, because inflation was running 8% while their portfolios fell 17%.

It didn't prove that rotation is "better" in any general sense. The strategy gives up upside during strong bull markets to gain protection during bear markets. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on what you're trying to accomplish with the money. Someone with a 50-year horizon and no risk tolerance issues may be perfectly fine with buy-and-hold QQQ. Someone in their late 60s with a decade of retirement spending ahead probably has a different answer.

One closing thought

The thing 2022 made obvious is that you have to know what your defensive plan actually is, ahead of time. "I'll hold through it" is a plan that works fine until it doesn't, and the moment it stops working is exactly the moment when changing plans is hardest. Most investors who panic-sold near the 2022 bottom didn't think of themselves as market-timers. They thought of themselves as long-term holders right up to the day they couldn't take it anymore.

A rules-based rotation isn't a magic shield. But it removes the decision from the moment of maximum stress. You don't have to think. You don't have to know what the Fed is going to do, what the next CPI print is going to say, whether tech earnings are coming in. The algorithm tells you whether the regime is hospitable to your equity exposure or it isn't. You execute accordingly.

For some people, that removes the worst part of investing — the part where you have to be smart and unemotional at exactly the moment you feel neither. For others, it's not worth the trade-off. There's no universal answer. But 2022 was a year where the trade-off looked very favorable indeed.
