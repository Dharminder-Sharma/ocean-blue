An honest page

When this might stop working.

Most marketing pages tell you why a strategy will keep succeeding. This one tells you what would make it fail. We'd rather you go in with eyes open than have a bad surprise twelve months in.

Every strategy has failure modes. The ones that pretend they don't are the ones to avoid. Here are the specific conditions under which Ocean Blue's algorithm would underperform, sometimes badly.

Choppy, range-bound markets

The single most reliable way to hurt a trend-following strategy is a market that goes nowhere with lots of short-term volatility. The algorithm exits on weakness, re-enters on strength, gets whipsawed repeatedly. Each round trip costs a small amount of return; many round trips over a year add up.

2010 and 2015 were exactly this kind of year. The strategy lost 2.84% in 2010 while QQQ gained 17.32%. It lost 10.81% in 2015 while QQQ gained 8.67%. These were the worst relative years in the 22-year backtest, and they happened during periods that, in retrospect, looked like continued bull markets but with multiple sharp pullbacks that the algorithm read as regime changes.

If the next several years happen to be choppy rather than trending, the strategy will underperform buy-and-hold materially. There's nothing in the rules that prevents this. Pattern recognition without prediction means accepting that the algorithm will be wrong in non-trending environments.

Persistently bullish years with shallow corrections

A market that grinds higher all year with only 5-10% pullbacks won't cost the strategy much, but it won't give it any opportunity to outperform either. Years like 2017 (QQQ +30.3%) and 2024 (QQQ +27.0%) saw the strategy roughly match but slightly trail buy-and-hold. In a 5-year stretch of pure uptrend with no regime breaks, the strategy will lag by something like 8-15 percentage points cumulatively. Not catastrophic, but also not a win.

Structural shifts in correlation patterns

The strategy uses TLT (long-term Treasuries) as the defensive rotation when equities are in a bear regime. This works when stocks and bonds are negatively correlated — which has been the dominant pattern since the early 1980s.

If we enter a sustained inflationary period where stocks and bonds are positively correlated for years on end, the TLT side of the rotation stops adding value. The algorithm partly addresses this by also checking whether TLT itself is above its 200-day moving average before rotating to it (and going to cash if not). 2022 was the test of this rule and it worked.

But the rules were developed in a world where bond bear markets were rare and brief. A multi-year bond bear market alongside an equity bear market would force the algorithm into extended cash positions, missing both equity rallies and bond rallies. The strategy would underperform a balanced portfolio that simply rode through.

Sudden regime breaks the rules don't catch

The algorithm uses moving averages that respond slowly by design — fast enough to catch most regime changes, slow enough to ignore noise. The trade-off is that very sudden moves can hurt the strategy because the algorithm takes several days to register the shift.

The 2020 COVID crash was an example. The S&P 500 fell 34% in five weeks — too fast for the algorithm to fully exit before most of the damage was done. The strategy still produced a positive 2020 (+41.7%, mostly from catching the rebound), but the timing was lucky. Had the COVID drawdown not been followed by an unprecedented recovery, the strategy could have ended the year deeply in the red.

A flash crash or geopolitical shock that produces a 25% equity drawdown over 48 hours would similarly catch the algorithm flat-footed. There's no rule fast enough to protect against truly sudden moves without also generating false signals constantly.

Decay of the underlying market patterns

The rules were developed by analyzing patterns in market behavior from 2004-2018, then validated on 2019-2026. If the underlying market patterns themselves shift — because of changes in market structure, participant composition, central bank behavior, regulation — the rules may stop working.

Specifically: if retail participation becomes much more dominant, if algorithmic trading shifts the short-term price dynamics, if regulatory changes alter how price discovery works, the technical signals that defined "regime" for the past 20 years may behave differently in the next 20.

There's no way to know in advance whether this is happening or has happened. The honest answer is that we'll find out by watching the strategy's live performance over years. If the live track record starts diverging meaningfully from backtest expectations — bigger drawdowns, more whipsaws, fewer correct regime calls — that's a signal that something has changed in the market and the rules may need revision.

What would make us pause the strategy

We have an explicit set of stopping criteria. If any of these happens, we'd publicly announce a pause while we investigate:

  • Maximum drawdown exceeds 40% (vs. backtest max of 28%)
  • Three consecutive years of significant underperformance versus buy-and-hold during periods that weren't just strong bull markets
  • Whipsaw frequency materially higher than backtest expectations over a 24-month window
  • Failure of the regime filter to identify a major drawdown (the algorithm holds through a 30%+ peak-to-trough decline)
  • Structural changes in market data quality, ETF availability, or related infrastructure that affect the rules' inputs

In a pause, we'd continue publishing paper signals while not charging subscribers. Subscribers could cancel and get prorated refunds for any unused paid period. We'd publish a public analysis of what we think changed and what we're doing about it.

This isn't a hypothetical we hope never happens. Strategies decay. Markets adapt. Edges get arbitraged away. We'd rather have a clear plan for the day the strategy stops working than pretend that day will never come.

The honest take on long-term durability

Simple, transparent rules tend to decay less than complex, opaque ones. The 200-day moving average has been a useful technical indicator for nearly 70 years. The combination of regime filter + volume confirmation has been studied in academic literature since the 1980s. None of this is proprietary insight — it's well-documented technical analysis applied with discipline.

That said, "less likely to decay than complex strategies" is not the same as "won't decay." Markets change. The strategy might keep working for 20 more years. It might stop working in 2027. We don't know in advance, and anyone who claims to know is selling you something.

What we can say is that if and when the strategy stops working, we'll tell you. Loudly, publicly, and with the data to back up the analysis. That commitment matters more than any specific claim about future performance.
