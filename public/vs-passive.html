For the index investor

Tactical rotation vs. buy-and-hold.

If you've spent years learning to ignore market timing advice — for very good reasons — you might reasonably wonder why this service exists. Fair question. Here's an honest answer.

First, what we're not arguing

We're not arguing that you should stop indexing. We're not arguing that active management beats passive in general. We're not arguing that this strategy will outperform a simple S&P 500 index fund over the next 30 years if you hold steady through the drawdowns. There's a good chance it won't, on absolute returns alone.

The data on passive vs. active is overwhelming and well-known. Most active strategies underperform their benchmarks net of fees. Most "tactical" funds are expensive ways to underperform the index. The Bogleheads framework — low-cost broad diversification, no market timing, focus on what you can control — has produced excellent outcomes for millions of investors and will keep producing excellent outcomes for millions more.

We don't dispute any of that.

The case where rotation might add value

There's a specific niche where simple rules-based rotation has earned its keep historically, and it has to do with the math of drawdown recovery and the behavior of real investors during deep bear markets.

The math. A 20% drawdown requires a 25% gain to recover. A 30% drawdown requires 43%. A 50% drawdown requires 100%. Drawdown recovery is geometric, not linear. If you can reduce maximum drawdown from 53% (buy-and-hold QQQ in 2008) to 28% (the strategy in 2008), the difference in recovery effort is large enough to compound into materially better long-run wealth, even with some give-up during bull markets.

The behavior. Most passive investors don't actually achieve the long-run returns of the passive funds they own. Dalbar's annual study has shown for decades that the average mutual fund investor underperforms the funds they're in by 2-4 percentage points per year, mostly because of poor buy/sell timing around drawdowns. The studies have methodological issues, but the underlying observation is real: holding through a 50% drawdown is much harder in practice than it sounds in theory.

A rotation strategy that reduces drawdowns by half makes "staying the course" easier. If you would have panic-sold near the 2009 bottom, the strategy's drawdown reduction may have kept you in the seat. The unmeasurable value of not capitulating at a bottom is real, even if it doesn't show up in any backtest.

Who shouldn't use this

You shouldn't use this if you've never sold during a drawdown. If you held through 2008, 2020, and 2022 without flinching, you don't need this. Your behavioral edge is already worth more than what the rotation strategy can add. Stay with what's working for you.

You shouldn't use this in a taxable account. The strategy fires about 25 trades per year. Most of those are short-term gains taxed as ordinary income. In a taxable account, the tax friction probably eliminates the strategy's edge over buy-and-hold. The math only works in IRAs and similar tax-advantaged accounts.

You shouldn't use this if you can't follow the signals. The strategy assumes you'll execute the rotations within a day or two of the signal. If you're going to override the algorithm's calls based on your own market views, you'll get worse results than if you just buy and hold. The whole point is to remove your discretion from the decision.

Who might find this useful

Someone within 10-15 years of retirement who has significant exposure to QQQ. The sequence-of-returns risk is real. A 50% drawdown five years before you need the money is materially different from a 50% drawdown 25 years before. Rotation as a risk-management tool makes more sense the closer you are to drawdown matters mattering.

An indexer who specifically uses QQQ for the tech exposure. If you've already decided that your IRA's growth tilt comes through QQQ rather than VTI or VOO, you're concentrated enough that drawdown management is worth considering. A 35% drawdown in your equity allocation hurts more if that allocation is QQQ than if it's a broadly diversified fund.

Someone who's been disciplined but knows they have a breaking point. You've held through ordinary drawdowns. You know you'd panic at a 60% drop. The strategy is essentially insurance against finding out where your panic point actually is. The premium is modest — about $240/year and some bull market underperformance — and the payoff is that you don't have to test your discipline in a once-in-a-decade crisis.

The Boglehead philosophy isn't wrong

The core insights — low costs matter, diversification matters, you can't predict markets, don't pay for active management you don't understand — are correct. We agree with all of them.

Where we'd push back is on the strict reading that all rules-based strategies are market timing and all market timing is bad. The 200-day moving average isn't a forecast. It's a description of what price has been doing for the past 200 days. Acting on it isn't predicting the future; it's responding to the present. That's a different activity from "buy when you think the market is bottoming."

Still, even if we're right about that distinction, the strategy has costs the simple-buy-and-hold approach doesn't have. Fees ($240/year + optional $60/year for SMS). Execution friction. Whipsaw periods like 2010 and 2015 where the strategy underperforms by double-digit percentage points. Bull markets where buy-and-hold simply runs and you give up some upside by being defensive too early.

Whether the trade-off is worth it depends on you specifically — your age, your tax situation, your behavioral tendencies, your risk tolerance, and your willingness to follow a discipline you didn't design. For some indexers it's a reasonable complement. For others it's a distraction from what's already working.

What we'd actually recommend

If you're a committed indexer and you're thinking about subscribing, don't replace your existing strategy. Carve off a piece of your IRA — say, 10-25% — and run Ocean Blue on it for a couple of years. See how it feels in practice. See whether you can actually follow the signals without second-guessing. See whether the drawdown protection feels worth the bull-market lag during a strong year.

If after two or three years you've concluded it's adding value, scale up. If it isn't, you've lost modest fees and learned something useful about your own preferences. Either way you haven't bet your retirement on it.

That's the recommendation we'd make to a careful indexer. It's not the most aggressive marketing pitch — "try a small allocation" is hardly the path to subscriber growth. But we'd rather have you start small and stay than start big and quit.
