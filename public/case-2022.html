2022, when nothing worked.

If 2008 was the test of rotating from stocks into bonds, 2022 was the test of recognizing that bonds weren't going to be your friend either. Tech fell 34%. Long-term Treasuries fell 30%. The 60/40 retirement portfolio had its worst year on record. Here's how a rules-based rotation handled it.

The setup, late 2021

Coming into 2022, the QQQ had just produced a stunning three-year run. From the COVID lows in March 2020, the index roughly tripled. Cheap money, work-from-home tech tailwinds, retail flooding into growth names. Everyone who'd been told to "just hold" had been spectacularly rewarded for it.

The Fed had been signaling a tightening cycle. Inflation was starting to look less transient. But the algorithm doesn't react to Fed signals or inflation data. It watches price.

And price had been making lower lows in tech for about a month when the regime broke.

The 2022 rotation sequence

January 18, 2022
QQQ → TLT
QQQ closed decisively below its 200-day moving average. volume signal confirmed the regime shift. The algorithm rotated to TLT, which was still in a marginal uptrend.
February 8, 2022
TLT → CASH
TLT itself broke below its 200-day moving average as bond yields surged on hawkish Fed commentary. The defensive asset wasn't defensive anymore. The algorithm moved to cash.
March-August 2022 — stayed in cash
Hold cash
Through the spring rally, the summer rally, and every "this is the bottom" call from financial media, the algorithm stayed in cash because QQQ remained below its 200-day moving average and the regime filter did not turn green.
August 24, 2022
CASH → QQQ (briefly)
The mid-August rally pushed QQQ briefly above its 200-day moving average. The algorithm re-entered. This was a whipsaw — within three weeks, QQQ rolled over again and the algorithm exited back to cash, taking a small loss on the round trip.
September 9 — December 2022
Cash through year-end
The algorithm spent the last four months of 2022 in cash as QQQ continued its decline to October lows and then chopped sideways. It did not catch the November-December rally because the regime filter remained red.

The math of 2022

The hypothetical strategy finished 2022 down approximately 7.30%. Buy-and-hold QQQ finished the year down approximately 32.58%. The 60/40 retirement portfolio finished down about 17.5%, its worst result in decades.

For a retirement-focused investor, the difference between an 8% drawdown and a 34% drawdown in one year is not abstract. A $500,000 IRA holding QQQ ended 2022 at roughly $331,000. The same $500,000 in the hypothetical strategy ended 2022 at roughly $459,000. That's $128,000 of preserved capital — about three years of moderate retirement spending in a typical household — earned by sitting in cash through a difficult market.

What 2022 taught us about TLT

Most rotation strategies assume that when equities go down, bonds will go up. That's been broadly true for 40 years. It wasn't true in 2022.

The reason is straightforward: the negative correlation between stocks and bonds is a function of the specific economic regime — declining inflation, accommodative central banks, falling real yields. When that regime ends, as it did in 2022, the correlation can flip positive. Both assets become rate-sensitive in the same direction.

The algorithm doesn't need to understand any of this. The rule is simple: don't hold a "defensive" asset that's itself in a downtrend. When TLT broke its 200-day moving average in February 2022, the algorithm stopped treating it as defensive and moved to cash. That single rule is what protected the strategy from the 60/40 disaster.

The cost of caution

The strategy gave back some performance in 2023. As QQQ ripped back from its 2022 lows, the algorithm didn't re-enter until price had decisively reclaimed its 200-day moving average — which didn't happen until early 2023, missing the late-2022 rally. Through 2023, the strategy returned about 44% to buy-and-hold's 55%. That's an 11-point lag.

This is the structural pattern of trend-following: you exit early, miss the bottom, re-enter late, miss the early rally. The cost of avoiding the catastrophic drawdown in 2022 was giving up some of the recovery in 2023.

The full two-year cycle still favored the strategy meaningfully. But the path was uncomfortable in both directions — sitting in cash while markets ran in summer 2022, then watching the early 2023 rally happen without you. This is one of the things that takes some getting used to as a subscriber. The strategy isn't trying to capture every move. It's trying to avoid the big drawdowns. The two objectives are not the same.

The honest read

2022 was a year where rotation did exactly what it's supposed to do. Caught the regime break. Avoided the worst of the equity drawdown. Recognized that bonds weren't a refuge this time. Stayed in cash long enough to matter.

But the strategy will not always look this good. There will be years where the algorithm rotates to cash and the market rallies without it. There will be whipsaws like the August 2022 round trip that cost a few percentage points. There will be moments where every other strategy is making money and ours is sitting on its hands.

The trade-off across a full market cycle is what matters. Avoiding 2022's full drawdown was worth the partial 2023 lag. Avoiding 2008's full drawdown was worth the partial 2009 lag. Avoiding the 2000-2002 dot-com crash (in the longer backtest) was worth the partial late-2003 lag. The pattern is consistent: what the strategy gives up in roaring bull markets, it more than recovers when the regime breaks.

The question for any subscriber is whether they can stay with the discipline through the periods when it costs them. If you can — and that's a real "if" — the math compounds in your favor over a long enough timeframe.
